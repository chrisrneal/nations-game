## The rule (docs/RULES.md 2.8, 3.3, 5.1)

Candidate (b) plus a reworked (c), plus one measurement fix. All three are needed;
each alone or in pairs was tried and is worse (see "What was tried").

1. **Structural baseline (b).** Every month the sim computes, from every nation's
   baseline path (no play), how much of the world's food and energy deficits the
   world's surpluses could cover (`structuralCoverSharePct` = 80% of the surplus
   counts as reachable). A nation's baseline output is its baseline potential less
   the shortfall penalty on the part of its deficit beyond that fair share. Japan's
   baseline now expects Japan to be short of energy in a short world.
2. **Trade gains by own imbalance (c, reworked).** Each side of a surplus-to-deficit
   trade gains `gainsFromTradeBp` x (value it cleared / value it could clear), capped
   at `gainsFromTradeBp` a month. What a nation can clear is its whole surplus plus
   its fair share of each deficit. A nation that trades away its whole surplus, or
   covers its fair share, grows 0.40% a month above its baseline whatever its size.
3. **Smoothed ownScore.** ownScore is the ratio of a 12-month exponential average of
   output to the same average of baseline output (`scoreSmoothingTicks` = 12), not
   the final month alone.

Tuned values (all inside their bands): `structuralCoverSharePct` 80 (new, band
50-100), `scoreSmoothingTicks` 12 (new, band 1-24), `shortfallPenaltyBpPerPct`
40 -> 35 (band 10-120), `gainsFromTradeBp` 40 (unchanged, band 5-40).

### Why this rule
- **Diagnosis first** (seeds 1001-1200, 200 games, old rules): India's capacity grew
  88% from trade over a game (Indonesia 47%, Russia 32%; Australia 4%, Brazil 6%).
  India exports food and imports energy. Under the old rule every sale that covered a
  small nation's whole food deficit paid India 0.40% of India's economy. At the
  same time Japan, Turkiye, Mexico, Korea, Germany and Egypt paid a 20-30% shortfall
  penalty even as traders, because the world cannot supply them. The trade bonus
  explains who won; the penalty explains who never could.
- **(b) is the D3-shaped fix for the penalty half.** Score stays "growth against the
  nation's own 2030 baseline x collective multiplier". The baseline just stops
  pretending a structural importer could have avoided a shortage nobody can
  avoid. It reads baseline paths only, so no play (including sabotage) moves anyone's
  baseline (RULES 5.3).
- **(c) as written, "volume relative to own output", was tried and rejected.** It
  moved the win to small open economies: Australia, Canada and Saudi Arabia topped
  15-25%, because their trade is a large share of a small output while the United
  States' and China's is small. Measuring each side against its *own imbalance*
  keeps the prompt's aim (importers and exporters gain at comparable rates, and the
  buyer's size no longer matters) without favouring any economy size.
- **Smoothing is needed because the metric rewards variance.** With (b) in place,
  nations whose final-month shortfall swings a lot (Germany +/-0.19 ownScore,
  Saudi Arabia +/-0.22) topped 20%+ of games while averaging below the leaders. A
  whole-game average halved that spread (Germany +/-0.09) but also diluted the trade
  gain, which builds up over the game, and cut the trade advantage to about +9%. A
  12-month average keeps the last year's gains in the score (+17.6% on every graded
  range) and still stops one month deciding the game. 6, 9, 12, 15 and 24 months
  were tried; 9-15 were equally good.

## Why it still fails: what is left

1. **The 200-game sample itself is noisy.** Each nation's share has a sampling
   spread of about +/-2.3 points. Even a perfectly fair game, where every nation
   has a 5.9% chance, shows its most frequent winner near 9% in a typical
   200-game run. To pass three ranges reliably the true top share needs to be about
   8-9%. This rule gets it to about 11-13%.
2. **In a short world, sellers are safe and buyers are lucky.** An exporter always
   finds buyers, so its score depends almost only on its own strategy (trader:
   high, anything else: 1.00). An importer's score depends on whether its sellers
   happen to be traders that game, and its hoarder and exploiter games score like
   its trader games. So importers get more, noisier chances to top, and exporters
   fewer, steadier ones. The balance point is narrow and moves with the seeds.
3. **The AI decides which importers get the scarce energy.** The greedy trader offers
   to the biggest deficits (docs/GAPS.md, prompt 07). China and India get offers but
   are too big to reach their fair share (they top 0-2%); Egypt and Saudi Arabia,
   whose deficits are small in volume and partly cheap food, beat their fair share often (they top
   10-13%). No rule in lanes S, D or H can change who the AI sells to.

## Decision for the owner

The threshold was not changed. The choices are:

1. **Keep this rule and fix the AI's offer spreading next (recommended).** It is
   the largest remaining driver and is already logged (prompt 07). Then re-grade on
   fresh seeds with the thresholds unchanged. Lane A.
2. **Keep this rule and waive the top-scorer criterion for Gate 1 in writing**,
   carrying it to Gate 2 (which re-runs it with archetypes). The worst nation is
   now at 12.5-15% instead of 34.5-45%, and every nation can win.
3. **Let background regions make offers** (RULES section 12 Q1), so the 15% of
   world energy they hold reaches market. It changes the scarcity itself and
   needs your answer to Q1 first.

A rule outside D3 (for example scoring nations against each other) could flatten
the table, but it would reward pulling a rival down, which D3 forbids. I did not
try one.
